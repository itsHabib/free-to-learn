# 05 · Decide when to abstain

A confident choice can still lead outside the target band. Build a calibration table for a named event and use it to decide when rules should take over.

## Name the event before measuring

A Choice distribution describes preference among action labels. Its confidence value is not automatically the probability of survival, completion or good pacing. **Calibration** compares a forecast with the frequency of a defined event. Our event is: the next resolved round ends alive and inside the announced pressure interval.

Every dataset row records an episode ID, revision, domain, synthetic choice score and event label. A label of one means the event occurred; zero means it did not. The label is computed after applying the player move and hazard. The score is generated before that transition. The corpus generator's rule is explicit: the fixture chooses scout when legal and emits one of three hash-selected scores. This deliberately makes a score that may carry little useful information about our event.

The event concerns one round under a particular action and player policy. It does not describe the probability of finishing the expedition. If you change the policy, player behavior or target schedule, the states you visit may change too. Calibration from one distribution of states does not automatically transfer to another.

## Fit a domain table on separate episodes

A **split** reserves whole episodes for a purpose: development explores a policy, calibration fits a mapping, and evaluation measures the frozen result. Adjacent rounds share history. Splitting by episode prevents related rounds appearing on both sides; it does not make rounds within an episode independent.

A **domain** here is the declared bot/profile combination. Keep a separate table for each domain. Ninety easy-domain successes and ten hard-domain failures at score 0.9 do not make the hard domain's event probability 0.9. That would transfer evidence across populations without support. In our supplied corpus, some cautious-player domains have no events at all. Inspect those rows in the table below rather than averaging them away.

Within each score bin, first group rows by episode. Compute each episode's event fraction, then average those fractions equally. This estimates the event rate for a uniformly selected eligible episode and then a uniformly selected row in its bin. Display both row counts and distinct episode counts; they answer different questions.

The supplied episodeInterval helper treats those bounded fractions as independent episode observations. Its conservative radius is sqrt(log(2/0.05)/(2*m)), where m is the number of episodes. Clip mean minus/plus the radius to [0,1]. This is a Hoeffding bound under independent, comparable episodes from the stated domain. It is not a claim that these deterministic fixture seeds establish a population interval, and it does not establish transfer to a changed policy or domain.

For ninety independent all-success episodes, the mean is 1 and the lower bound is about 0.857. For ten all-failure episodes in another domain, the mean is 0 and the upper bound is about 0.429. Repeating twelve successful rounds from one episode still gives m=1, and the selector abstains. The minimum of ten episodes is a declared teaching policy; the interval, not the count alone, must also support the threshold.

For completion practice, put six successes and four failures in one episode and compute its fraction. Then add a second episode with one failure. Compare the equal-episode mean with the pooled row mean. Explain why duplicating the first episode's rows under the same identities must be rejected rather than treated as more evidence.

## Score forecasts and choose coverage

The **Brier score** is the average squared error between forecast and binary label. Lower is better for this event. For forecasts [0.9, 0.9, 0.6, 0.6] and labels [1, 0, 1, 0], the squared errors sum to 1.34, giving 0.335. A constant forecast of 0.5 scores 0.25 on those labels. A confident forecast can therefore lose to a simple constant.

A constant prevalence forecast has a limitation: it does not distinguish easy from difficult cases. Report its score without calling it a universally useful selector. Compare mapped forecasts with both raw scores and the calibration prevalence baseline on the separate evaluation data. The generated corpus includes six bot/profile domains; report counts for each instead of hiding a weak slice in an overall average.

**Abstention** means declining to use a proposal and executing the rules fallback. The selector abstains for unknown domains, insufficient bins, invalid proposals and intervals below threshold. **Coverage** is the fraction of proposals used. A high threshold can yield zero coverage. That is an honest result if the evidence is weak; do not lower the threshold after viewing final evaluation just to produce an impressive chart.

## Implement and transfer

Complete fitCalibration, brier and select in confidence.mjs. Start with counts, then add probabilities and intervals, then the abstention reasons. The check rejects evaluation rows, duplicate episode/revision identities and cross-domain borrowing. A positive control also requires accepting a genuinely supported bin, so always abstaining is not a complete selector. Inspect episode families yourself; a field called calibration cannot establish independence.

For a fresh case, reverse the relationship: high scores now accompany failures. Keep the original threshold frozen and predict what happens. Refit only on a new calibration split, assign it a new identity, and compare separately. In module 08 you will distinguish a faulty formula from a changed domain. No result in this lesson establishes how any real provider behaves.
