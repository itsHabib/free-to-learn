# 9 · Capstone: a scene you have not seen, under a budget

In this lesson you draw halls you haven't seen, with the right triangle at every pixel, while launching no more shader warps and moving no more bytes than two stated budgets allow.

Your GPU from unit 8 draws every hall correctly. It exceeds the shading budget on every hall, and meeting that budget needs a step the course hasn't shown, built from parts you already have. This lesson gives the problem, the two budgets and their reasons, and the commands that measure your renderer against them. It doesn't give the method.

**Code scope:** you write `renderVisible(ctx)` in `project/gpu/capstone.mjs`, every line your own. There is no listing and no supplied route. Everything you need from earlier units is already in your project.
**Time:** about 6 minutes to read. Building takes longer than in the earlier units. Neither is measured with learners yet.

## Two halls to practise on

`hallScene(seed)` in `scenes/hall.mjs` builds a hall of arches. A row of stone screens, each pierced by a round-headed arch, stands one behind another down the hall, with a stair and a back wall at the far end. At the top of the stair a doorway opens on the night, where no triangle covers the picture. Each screen carries a tablet laid twice in the same place, first in gilt and then in plain stone. The seed changes the number of arches, their spacing, size and colour, and where the camera stands. The screens, the stair and the back wall arrive from the back of the hall to the front. The floor arrives last, after the screens that hide most of it, so arrival order alone doesn't give the right picture. `hall.asm` shades them, texture times colour times light, 13 instructions. The pictures are 160 by 120.

## Five halls you have not seen

The check also draws five other halls, seeds the course never shows. Your course folder holds no picture of them, only **fingerprints**, which hold, for each 16 by 16 tile, a hash of the triangle id at every pixel, and the sum of each colour channel over the tile. The ids must match exactly, the nearest triangle at every pixel, and the background where no triangle covers it. Each tile's colour sums must be within 64 levels per channel. The halls were chosen so that the two nearest surfaces at any pixel differ in depth by far more than float rounding, so no correct renderer can lose an id to rounding. There is one exception, exact on purpose. The two copies of each tablet share every corner, so they land at exactly the same depth at every pixel.

There, lesson 3's equal-depth rule decides which id the pixel holds.

Question 9a. Two triangles cover a pixel at exactly the same depth. Which one's id must the pixel hold? (a) the one drawn first, (b) the one drawn second, (c) whichever covers more of the tile, (d) either, the check accepts both.

```sh
node lab.mjs predict project 9a YOUR_ANSWER
```

The one drawn first. Under `LESS` a later fragment at the same depth fails the test, so every tablet shows gilt, and the fingerprints require it.

## Colour only from shadeWarp

As the contract above says, `shadeWarp` is the only way to a colour, and it counts every warp it launches. The check requires every visible pixel's colour to be one that `shadeWarp` returned for the triangle the pixel shows, so the budget can't be met by making colours some other way.

## The shading budget

A warp runs the whole shader on every lane it holds, 13 instructions and a texture read on each hall, and issues each instruction once however many lanes are active, so warps launched are what shading costs. The budget is measured against **visible warps**. For each 4 by 2 block of the final picture, count the different triangles that own at least one of its pixels, and add those counts up over all the blocks. A renderer may launch at most 1.25 times the visible warps of each hall.

Question 9b. In the final picture, the eight pixels of one block show triangles 3, 3, 7, 7, 7, the background, 3 and 9. How many visible warps does that block add?

## The memory budget

A renderer may move no more bytes than unit 8's tiled renderer moves on the same hall, counting depth, colour, binning and texture reads. It stops a renderer from trading memory traffic for less shading. Storing a per-pixel result to memory and reading it back costs bytes, and on a GPU those bytes cost bandwidth and power.

<summary>Hint 1, where the warps go</summary>

Most of those warps shade fragments that a later triangle covers: a pixel near the camera is shaded for each screen behind it that passes the depth test when it arrives, and only the last shading survives. Arrival order is no guide to what is nearest here, because the floor arrives last and the tablets tie.

</details>

Inside one tile, which triangle is nearest at each pixel can be found before any shading, because the depth test needs only depth, not colour.

<details>
<summary>After the capstone, what you built</summary>

What you built is called deferred shading inside a tile. Tile-based deferred renderers do the same in hardware. They bin the frame's triangles first, then for each tile they resolve, without shading, which triangle is nearest at every pixel, and only then run the fragment shader, once per visible pixel. Other tile-based designs, common in phones and tablets, get part of that saving instead by testing depth before shading, in arrival order, as your renderer from unit 5 to unit 8 does. On a tile-based deferred renderer, overdraw that arrives back to front costs almost nothing, where a plain immediate renderer pays a shading pass per layer. Surfaces that mix their colour with what lies behind them, or leave some of their pixels undrawn, still have to be shaded in order on any of these designs. As in unit 8, every triangle has to be binned and read back per tile, so a frame of many tiny triangles can cost more than it saves.

</details>
