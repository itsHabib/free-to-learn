## How surprising is 80 percent?

Imagine two reports, one with 8 wins out of 10, the other with the 196 out of 245 you just counted. Both say 80%. A classical strategy that wins each independent, fair-settings round with probability 0.75 has about a 53% chance of reaching at least eight in ten. Its chance of reaching at least 196 in 245 is about 3.9%. The tail probability asks how often that baseline reaches a result this high or higher. It doesn't give the probability that the equipment is classical.

![Two bar charts of how many rounds a strategy that wins each round with probability 3/4 would win. With 10 rounds, the bars from 8 to 10 are shaded: it reaches 8 or more 52.6% of the time. With 245 rounds, the bars from 196 up are shaded, far out in the right tail: 3.9%. Both reports are 80%.](figures/two-tails.svg)

The `--uniform-null` flag computes this tail probability against a 0.75 baseline, assuming a fixed trial count, no communication between the stations, and fresh, uniformly random settings independent of the devices. Use it when you can justify those assumptions. The deliberately skewed fixture breaks the setting assumption, so its tail would mislead. The paper documents the trial-selection rule, timing, measurement choices and a statistical treatment of possible device memory. A spreadsheet of counts cannot establish how the lab chose which trials to keep. [Methods and statistical analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.05949)
