# Can the fourth card win?

**0:00** Alice and Bob sit in separate rooms. Each receives one card, marked zero or one.

**0:06** They can agree on a plan beforehand. Once the cards arrive, each must answer with one bit, without seeing the other card.

**0:14** For three deals, their answers have to match. If both cards show one, their answers have to differ.

**0:21** Suppose they always answer zero. Which deal costs them a point? Pause and pick it.

**0:29** One and one. They give matching answers just when the referee needs different answers.

**0:35** What if Alice alone changes her answer on a one? Watch the lost point move.

**0:40** She saves the last deal. Now she loses when her card is one and Bob's is zero. Could a cleverer plan win all four?

**0:49** Put each possible answer on a pin. To win the first deal, Alice on zero must equal Bob on zero.

**0:56** To win the next two, Bob on one must match Alice on zero, and Alice on one must match Bob on zero.

**1:03** Follow the green path. All four answers must now match. The last deal asks the two ends to differ.

**1:10** With independent fair cards, every deal has a one in four chance. A local plan can win at most three quarters on average. Mixing plans cannot improve that limit.

**1:22** A quantum pair offers a different move. Let each card choose the angle at which its holder measures one half of an entangled pair.

**1:30** At these angles the ideal quantum win rate is about eighty five percent. Each answer alone still looks random. The pattern appears when you compare the two logs.
