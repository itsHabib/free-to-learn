# 14 · Meet the limit, then detect

The searching client sends legal inputs: one fire a round, an angle and a power in range, in order. Your admission admits every one of them, as it should, because an honest player could send each of them too. With the drawn view it still hits about 65% of its shots. No rule about a single message can stop it, because no single message is wrong.

The session, though, is unusual. Over twelve rounds the searching client fires at nearly the same moment every round, sends one message per round where people send several, and lands closer than people do. By the end of this lesson your `detect/` files will turn a session into a handful of numbers, score it, set a threshold on development sessions (the ones you design on) for a stated budget of false flags, freeze that threshold, measure it once on held-out sessions (kept back until the end), and work out what its flags would mean in a game where one session in 100 is a cheat. A flag here is the detector's mark on a session, not the flag a shell aims at, and it's a request for a person to look. Nothing in this course bans anyone.

Part one's unit M7 built the same discipline for the courier arena: case-level counts, frozen settings, and rates with their denominators.

## The corpus

`public/corpus.mjs` plays sessions on the defended server: lesson 12's rules, the drawn view of lesson 13, six kinds of legal player and the searching client. It plays them with the course's own server code, not yours, so every learner's corpus is the same.

| Kind | Who |
| --- | --- |
| `novice`, `skilled`, `expert` | three skill levels on an ordinary connection (30 ms each way) |
| `highLatency` | skilled players 250 ms away |
| `jitter` | skilled players whose delay varies by up to 60 ms, so messages overtake each other |
| `reconnects` | skilled players who lose the connection two or three times a session, for 2 to 6 s |
| `searcher` | the searching client, labelled `cheat` |

`makeCorpus({ split: 'dev' })` gives 1,100 sessions: 1,000 legal and 100 searcher. `makeCorpus({ split: 'heldout' })` gives 2,200 more, 2,000 legal and 200 searcher, played from seeds the dev split never uses. Each session comes as a case, `{ id, label, kind, record, rounds }` (API.md, "A case"). You fit everything on dev, choosing your settings and threshold from its sessions alone. Held-out is read once, at the end.

The legal players are models written for this course. They have reaction times, aim with noise and a personal bias, learn from where their last shell landed, and press keys at a human pace. They aren't people, and the lesson comes back to what that means.

## Features

A **session** is the unit. A detector that judged single shots would see thousands of them, and a few hundred lucky ones among honest players would look like a crowd of cheats. Counting sessions gives each session one decision, so the denominator counts sessions, not shots.

Write `features(c)`, with the definitions in API.md: hit rate, median miss, median time to fire and its spread, median inputs per round, how regular the gaps between inputs are (`cadenceCV`, their coefficient of variation, the standard deviation over the mean), and how a miss relates to the next one. Use only `c.record.inputs` and `c.rounds`. The checks give your function nothing else, so a detector can't read a session's label or name. Check `14.features` compares your features with the definitions on sessions of every kind. Run it alone with `node lab.mjs check project 14 --case 14.features`, because the whole unit includes `14.heldout`, which measures your detector on sessions of its own and belongs after the freeze.

## The adapted searcher

A cheat whose sessions get flagged adapts. `recordings/` holds sessions of two adapted searchers, recorded once by the course's author with a tool the course doesn't ship. `delay` uses the searcher's aim but sends it the way an expert would, with several inputs a round at a human pace, about two and a half seconds into the round. Its timing and its input counts look like an expert's, and only its aim gives it away. `delay-misses` does the same and also misses on purpose.

The recordings are labelled examples of an adapted cheat, not held-out sessions, so run `node lab.mjs recordings project` whenever you like while you design your score. Check `14.recordings` requires your detector to flag at least 12 of the 60 `delay` sessions, so a score built on timing alone won't pass.

For comparison, the course's worked detector, described in the next section, gives this table, in which recall is the share of a set's sessions it flags:

```
  set           sessions           hit rate  flagged  missed  recall
  delay               60    68.1% (490/720)       22      38   0.367
  delay-misses        60    31.1% (224/720)        0      60   0.000
```

It still flags 22 of the 60 `delay` sessions, because they land far closer than experts do. It flags none of the `delay-misses` sessions, because they miss about as often as an expert.

Question 14c: the `delay-misses` sessions hit 31.1% of their shots, and the dev split's experts hit 27.5%. If your detector flags none of them, how many percentage points of hit rate has the cheat bought over an expert, to the nearest whole point?

```
node lab.mjs predict project 14c YOUR_ANSWER
```

Detection falls when the cheat adapts, and so does what the cheat gets for cheating. A defence doesn't have to catch every adapted cheat to work, as long as it makes cheating cost more than it buys.

## A score and a budget

Write `score(features, settings)`. The rule is yours. One rule that works takes, for each feature, how far the session sits from the legal median in units of a robust scale, its robust z-score, signs it so that the suspicious side is positive, caps it so that one wild feature can't decide alone, and sums them. The robust scale is 1.4826 times the median absolute deviation of the legal sessions, and the median absolute deviation is the median of their distances from the legal median. The factor makes the scale match the standard deviation when values are normally distributed, and it's robust because a few extreme sessions barely move it, where they'd drag a mean and a standard deviation with them. `node lab.mjs features project` prints the legal median and robust scale of each feature for exactly this, rounded to three decimals, which is precise enough to copy into `detect/settings.json`. Times, counts and distances are skewed (a few very slow players, a few huge misses), so compare them as `Math.log1p(value)`. Leave out a feature whose legal robust scale is 0, such as `shots`. Almost every session fires in all 12 rounds, so the median absolute deviation is 0 and dividing by it means nothing. The course's worked detector gives every feature a weight of 1 and a cap of 10. A much lower cap clips the `delay` sessions' aim scores, the part of their score that gives them away.

A threshold turns a score into a decision, and a session is flagged when its score is at or above the threshold. The decision has a cost, because every flagged legal session is a false flag, a person asked to look at a player who did nothing. So choose the threshold from that cost. The budget here is one false flag in 500 legal sessions. Write `chooseThreshold(legalScores, budget)`: the smallest of the scores such that the share of scores at or above it is at most the budget.

## Base rates

Recall is the share of cheat sessions a detector flags, the false positive rate is the share of legal sessions it flags, and precision is the share of flagged sessions that are cheats. Recall and the false positive rate belong to the detector, but precision also depends on how many sessions are cheats. When cheats are rare, even a small false positive rate fills the flagged list with legal players.

Write `count(decisions)`, `evaluate(cases, settings)` and `precisionAt(baseRate, recall, fpr)`. `count` turns one decision per session, `{ label, flagged }`, into the counts tp (flagged cheats, true positives), fn (cheats not flagged, false negatives), fp (flagged legal sessions, false positives) and tn (legal sessions not flagged, true negatives), with the three rates. `evaluate` makes one decision per case with your score and threshold and counts them. `precisionAt` takes the base rate, the share of sessions that are cheats, with the recall and the false positive rate (fpr).

Question 14b: a detector catches 90% of cheats and flags 1% of legal sessions. One session in 100 is a cheat. What share of flagged sessions are cheats, to the nearest whole percent? Commit:

```
node lab.mjs predict project 14b YOUR_ANSWER
```

That's why the budget is a false-flag budget, and why it's so much smaller than 1%. Check `14.counting` tests `count` and `precisionAt`, including a denominator of 0.

## Freeze, then held-out

Question 14e: why must the threshold come from the dev split, and not from the sessions you will report? (a) held-out sessions are harder (b) a threshold chosen on the sessions it's then measured on is measured too kindly (c) the dev split has more cheats (d) `chooseThreshold` accepts only dev scores

```
node lab.mjs predict project 14e YOUR_ANSWER
```

Everything so far used dev sessions. So freeze first:

```
node lab.mjs freeze project
node lab.mjs heldout project
```

`freeze` records the hashes of your settings and detector files in your receipt, the records lab.mjs keeps in your project's `attempts/` folder. `heldout` refuses to run unless they still match, evaluates the 2,200 held-out sessions, and keeps its first result in the receipt. Any later run is added after it, marked as later. If you change your detector after seeing held-out results, the receipt shows it. Check `14.frozen` confirms that your threshold meets the budget on the dev split, and check `14.heldout` measures your frozen detector on held-out sessions of its own.

The course's own worked detector, a capped sum of robust z-scores like the one described above, fitted on dev to threshold 15.319, gives on the held-out split:

```
  tp 200  fn 0  fp 1  tn 1999
  searcher recall       1.000 (200 of 200 flagged)
  false flags           1 of 2,000 legal sessions (false positive rate 0.0005)
  precision at 1 in 100 0.953
```

The one legal session it flags is an expert's. Yours will differ, but the first number you get is still the one you report.

Zero or one false flag in 2,000 model players isn't evidence that real players would be flagged at that rate. Players the models don't cover (a player on a phone, a player with one hand, a professional) can sit anywhere.

## Check

```
node lab.mjs check project 14
```

Check `14.heldout` plays 2,200 sessions of its own, with the held-out split's mix of kinds, and requires your frozen settings to flag at least 90% of its searcher sessions and at most 15 of its 2,000 legal sessions. That allowance is wider than the budget on purpose, because a threshold set exactly at one in 500 on 1,000 dev sessions flags somewhat more or fewer on fresh sessions by chance, and the check shouldn't fail a correct detector for that. Your own held-out number, in your receipt, is the one to report.
