## What you know about Lob II

Levels 8, 9 and 10 are three builds of **Lob II**, a game of the same kind as Lob, made separately. `game/lob2.wasm` is the only part of it you get, and no lesson says anything more about it until your engine has cleared level 8. It's played with the same six keys and shows the same kind of status line and messages, which is what lets your controls probe, `parseStatus` and `fire` work on it. The page drives it through two functions with the same names as Lob's, `game_key` and `game_tick`. Everything else may differ, and finding it is your engine's work.

Its three levels are listed by `node lab.mjs level --help`, with their budgets:

## A kit from another game

The engine keeps only the controls. A part your judges confirmed on Lob was confirmed on shots fired in Lob, on Lob's screen, against Lob's frames, and that says nothing about a game the judges never saw. The same goes for every address in the kit.

`gameId(host)` is given in your starter. It returns a short string made from the bytes of the game's file, so two hosts give the same string exactly when they run the same file. Every build of Lob is one file and has one id, and Lob II has another. `playLevel` needs a step in front of its loop that empties a kit from another game:

1. Take `gameId(host)`.
2. If `kit.game` isn't that id, the kit belongs to another game, or is empty. Delete `statusLine`, `message`, `found`, `shots`, `fit`, `physics`, `reader` and `mover`.
3. Set `kit.game` to the id.

Why each entry goes:

- The addresses of the status line and the message, and the found addresses, are places in another file's memory.
- The practice shots would feed the nearest-shot aim that `aimFor` falls back on.
- The fit and the physics carry the id of the game they were confirmed on, and `checkKit` wouldn't rely on them here, but nothing should be left for a fallback to pick up.
- `kit.reader` and `kit.mover` keep only `which`, the index of the confirmed candidate, and the engine rebuilds them from `analyse(host)`. Carried to another game, `which` would name a candidate from the new game's analysis that no test on that game has confirmed. `checkKit` tests a reader at the start of every level, but it doesn't watch the flag again once the kit has a motion, so a carried mover would be used untested.

The controls stay in the kit, and nothing relies on them. With the status line's address gone, `controlsHold` can't confirm them, so the first problem on the new game is the controls, and the probe from module 01 works them out again in 2 rounds. After that the loop you wrote in module 06 does the rest. It finds a kit that knows nothing about this game, and asks, one problem at a time, for what the level needs.

## The engine never runs the game

Your engine reads the game's file, decodes it, lifts its functions and evaluates what it lifted on a copy of memory. It never runs the file. It doesn't make a `WebAssembly.Module` or an `Instance` of it, and calls no `instantiate` or `compile`.

A copy of the game, run beside the real one, would answer everything. Feed it a setting, read where the shell lands, and aim with that. It would work on any game and explain nothing, because the engine would know no more about the game than before. Every finding in your kit is something your passes worked out from the code and a test confirmed against the game. The referee's rules for the course forbid running the file. The check `08-no-run` fails a file of yours that uses WebAssembly, looks a name up on `globalThis` by a computed key, or imports `vm`, `worker_threads`, `child_process`, `cluster` or `wasi`, the modules that can start a fresh copy of JavaScript or, in the case of `wasi`, run a WebAssembly program, and the checks run your files with WebAssembly removed from the global object. That stops the plain ways to run a copy, which is all an honest engine meets, but it isn't a proof that no program could find another.

`flagPath` runs six stores of the frame function on a copy of memory, but they are stores your passes found and the motion judge tested, and your engine can say what each one does. It never hands the game's bytes to WebAssembly.

## What your passes found in Lob II

Your passes found 2 stores in the flag's slice, and the first option your engine ran was the controls, because the game step had deleted the status line's address. The log shows the order of the work: the controls, the state, the flag, the aim, each as `checkKit` found it missing, and each confirmed against the game before it went into the kit. Here is what the passes found, from `node lab.mjs lift game_tick ./work --game lob2`, `lift game_key` and `xrefs` with the same flag, and the sentences your engine logged.

The state sits behind two pointers. The reader's sentence starts `The state is the struct at *0x14120+4+0`. Read a place like `*0x14120+4+36` from the left. The word at 0x14120 is an address, field +4 of the struct there holds a second address, and field +36 of the struct at that second address is the flag's word. Reading it takes three loads, the last of them the flag's word itself. Through more than one pointer `place` writes a +0 out, which is why the state's own address reads `*0x14120+4+0`. Written `*0x14120+4`, it would be field +4 of the first struct. The cross-references show that first struct. Its field +4 is read by `func5`, `func6`, `game_key`, `game_tick` and the drawing functions `g0` to `g5`, and written only by `func5`. Nothing in your passes assumed one pointer. A load's address is an expression, and that expression can be another load, to any depth, so the lifter, `place`, `resolve` and the evaluator all follow the chain as far as it goes.

The values are stored with a key added. The angle's stepper decodes `asfloat((i*0x14120+4+100 - i*0x14120+4+8))`, so the game stores the value plus the word at +8 and gets the value back by subtracting it. Your `decoding` names that `add`, and the rest follows from it. The reader decodes the angle at +100, the power at +96, the flag at +36 and the wind at +56 the same way, and `sayReader` says `values stored with the word at +8 added`.

The cannon is a constant at +104. The launch reads +104, and no store your lifter found writes it through the pointer, so your constant tracking keeps it as a constant. Your physics' sentence says what it holds: `+104 (no store through the pointer writes it; 64 now)`. The launch store shows what it is:

```
f*0x14120+4+64 = ((26 * cos((ANGLE * 0.0174532924))) + f*0x14120+4+104)
```

with the angle's decoded expression written as ANGLE. The shell's x starts 26 × cos(angle) past the value at +104, at the tip of a barrel 26 paces long, so +104 is the cannon's x. Lob's was +20, which held 80. A physics that kept Lob's 80 would predict every landing on Lob II 16 paces too far, and its shots would fall 16 paces short.

The flight has its own constants. The flight is 6 stores again, the landing at +80 among them. The shell's upward speed loses 4 paces a second every frame, which is 240 a second squared, where Lob's lost 5.00000048, 300. The wind is multiplied by 15, where Lob's was by 12. Your simulator runs the stores it found, so it never needed these numbers written down. Three check shots each landed within a pace of the simulated landing, after the same number of frames.

The flag sways on a sine. The flag's slice is 2 stores:

```
f*0x14120+4+40 = ((f*0x14120+4+44 * 0.0166666675) + f*0x14120+4+40)
i*0x14120+4+36 = (i*0x14120+4+8 + asbits((f*0x14120+4+48 +
  (f*0x14120+4+52 * sin(((f*0x14120+4+44 * 0.0166666675) +
  f*0x14120+4+40))))))
```

The first moves a phase at +40 on by a rate, +44, times a sixtieth of a second, every frame. The second stores the flag as a centre, +48, plus a size, +52, times the sine of the phase, with the key added. Neither store has a guard, so Lob II's flag moves on every build, the plain one included. `flagPath` runs the two stores with `Math.sin` for the import `sin`, and the path held for 600 frames over 2 rounds.

None of the passes the lessons ask for was written for Lob II, and none of them names an address, a value from either game, or a game. Each one recognises a shape: a stepper, a decode, the stores that use `sin` and `cos`, the slice that flies the shell, the comparison that tests a hit, the slice that moves the flag. The shapes are the same in a game made separately, and the numbers come out of the code.

## What the measurement shows, and what it does not

It shows these things, on these two games:

- Your heuristic, the cheapest option no evidence rules out, passed every level within its budget.
- A picker that ignores the evidence fails where the option it prefers is ruled out and the budget is tight, as option 0 did on level 3.
- Random picks failed levels 8, 9 and 10 in 5, 8 and 6 of the 20 climbs, every time through the search on the new game. Losing level 8 for want of rounds for the physics lost levels 9 and 10 as well.
- An assumption that held on Lob can change what an option costs on another game. The search assumes a still flag, and on Lob II 158 of 500 random orders of its experiments took 8 rounds instead of 4.
- No decision model's pick beat your heuristic on any level. The smaller model searched on level 8 where the rule read the state, and got away with it because its search took 4 rounds and the budget had room. The larger one picked as the rule did every time. The one time a model decided when to fire, the larger one on level 9, it said wait 72 times in 79, and its shots hit because the engine fires a plan made from what it has just seen when time runs out, not because waiting helped.

It does not show these things:

- Whether a picker could beat your rule on other menus. On these menus the cheapest option no evidence rules out always worked, and `Fire now` was a good answer every time, so no picker could beat the rule here, and the measurement could only show a picker matching it or losing to it. A menu where the cheapest option the evidence leaves sometimes fails is where a picker could earn its place.
- How the models would do over many climbs. Each ran one climb and two extra levels at temperature 0, which isn't enough to see how much their results vary. Whether a model decides at all rests on three timings. The larger model was over the budget on every level of its climb, and under it on level 9 played again from option 0's kit.
- How a decision model built for this work would do. `llama3.2:1b` and `qwen2.5:7b` are general small models standing in for one. Nothing here measures a model trained to read this kind of menu.
- That small models pick worse than rules in general. On these menus your rule had everything a model saw, the costs and the evidence, already in the form it needed, because the same code wrote both. A menu whose options can't be priced or ruled out by code is a different question.
- Fine differences between pickers. The single climbs answered two to seven repair menus each, and the 20 random climbs 72 between them, too few to tell close pickers apart.

Code earned the levels: passes that recognise shapes, tests that confirm a finding before the kit relies on it, a loop that finds what is broken, and a rule that reads the evidence.

## What you built

Your engine met a game nobody had described to it and climbed it with the same passes and the same loop. The defences of both games gave the same result. Moving and scrambling a game's values cost the engine one reading of the code, random numbers made on the client were predicted from the code, and the one thing no reading recovered was data the client was never sent.

Real tools do this work at full scale: Ghidra, whose analysis scripts lift and search compiled code as your passes do, and Cheat Engine, which scans a running program's memory as your module 02 search does. This course works only on the two games it ships, each defence paired with the step that gets past it, and nothing in it teaches getting past anyone else's protections.
